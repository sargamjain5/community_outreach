The three traps

Three ways the internet fools you

@free_gadgets_hub
FREE laptops for ALL students from Monday!! Forward to 10 friends to confirm.

1 · The fake claim

Made up from scratch. It sounds exciting, so people pass it on.

Clues: no official source, a huge promise, and pressure to forward. Check: the organisation's real website or notice board.

2 · Right picture, wrong story

A real photo or video, but from another time or place.

Clues: no date, no place, no original poster. Check: reverse-search the image to find its earliest copy.

3 · AI & edited media

Images or video made or changed by software to look real.

Clues: impossible details and no trusted report anywhere. Check: does any reliable outlet or fact-checker mention it?

All three examples are invented for this talk. Each one tries to make you share before you think.

Case file · India · 2025 monsoon

Real floods. False “footage.”

@viral_clips_demo Reel
Misleading clip
Stylised illustration of the kind of post involved. Not the original clip.

During India's 2025 monsoon, AI-generated flood videos were shared as if they were genuine footage. The floods were real. The specific viral clips were misleading.

Fact-checkers study a clip frame by frame for things that do not make physical sense: odd motion, warped objects, details that change.
They look for the original post and earlier copies, and for any trusted report that matches the footage.
Fact-checking does not say “nothing happened.” It asks: is this specific video evidence of that event? The AFP Fact Check, linked below, covers the specific clips.
